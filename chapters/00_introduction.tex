\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:intro}

The discovery of the Higgs boson at the LHC~\cite{ATLAS:2012yve,CMS:2012qbp} completed the particle
content of the Standard Model, and the measurements that followed have confirmed its predictions
across all of its sectors. No additional particle has been observed, either at the LHC or in the
broad range of lower-energy experiments that probe the same interactions indirectly. This success
coexists with a list of questions that the Standard Model leaves unanswered. Some are observational,
such as the nature of dark matter, the origin of neutrino masses, and the asymmetry between matter
and antimatter. Others concern the structure of the theory itself, such as the smallness of the
CP-violating angle of quantum chromodynamics, the hierarchical pattern of fermion masses and
mixings, and the stability of the electroweak scale against large quantum corrections. It is
therefore widely held that the Standard Model is the low-energy description of a more fundamental
theory, whose new degrees of freedom appear at a scale $\Lambda$ larger than the energies explored
so far.

If that scale lies beyond direct reach, the new dynamics can only be seen through its indirect
effects, and these take a universal form. At energies $E \ll \Lambda$, the heavy states cannot be
produced, and their exchange reduces to local interactions among the light fields, suppressed by
powers of $E/\Lambda$. Whatever the underlying theory is, its low-energy imprint is therefore
captured by a finite number of coefficients multiplying higher-dimensional operators built out of
the light fields. The search for physics beyond the Standard Model is thereby recast as the
determination of these coefficients, and the effective field theory that organizes them becomes the
common language in which direct searches, precision measurements, and model building can be compared.

That language is useful only if two requirements are met. The coefficients are generated at the
scale $\Lambda$ and measured at much lower energies, often several orders of magnitude apart, so
their evolution between the two scales must be under control. At the same time, the coefficients
cannot take arbitrary values, because the effective theory must be the low-energy limit of a
consistent quantum field theory, which restricts the region they may occupy before any measurement
is made. This thesis is concerned with both requirements. Its second part computes how the
coefficients evolve through the anomalous dimensions of the corresponding operators and studies
the structure of those anomalous dimensions. Its third part determines which regions of coefficient
space are compatible with the unitarity, analyticity, and locality of the $S$-matrix. Both parts rely
on the same set of tools, the on-shell methods for scattering amplitudes.

\bigskip

Both requirements are best stated within the logic of effective field theory, which is older than
the Standard Model and not specific to it~\cite{Weinberg:1978kz,Georgi:1993mps,Manohar:2018aog}.
Physics at a given scale depends on physics at much higher scales only through a small number of
parameters, and the effects of heavy states decouple from low-energy observables as inverse powers
of their masses~\cite{Appelquist:1974tg}. The Fermi theory of the weak interaction is the classic
example. Below the $W$-boson mass, the exchange of a $W$ boson between two fermion currents reduces
to a four-fermion contact interaction with a coupling proportional to $g^2/m_W^2$. The structure of
the underlying gauge theory is visible only through the pattern of the effective couplings, and the
violation of unitarity by the contact interaction at high energies signals that new degrees of
freedom must appear, as the $W$ boson eventually did.

Applied to physics beyond the Standard Model, this reasoning leads to writing the most general
Lagrangian compatible with the light field content and with the symmetries assumed at low energies,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro_Leff}
    \mathcal L_{\rm EFT} = \mathcal L_{\rm SM} + \sum_i \frac{c_i}{\Lambda^{[\mathcal O_i]-4}}\,\mathcal O_i\,,
\end{equation}
where the operators $\mathcal O_i$ have mass dimension $[\mathcal O_i]>4$ and the dimensionless
Wilson coefficients $c_i$ encode the unknown dynamics at the scale $\Lambda$. The expansion is
organized by dimensional analysis. An operator of dimension $d$ contributes to an observable at the
energy $E$ with a relative size $(E/\Lambda)^{d-4}$, so that to any given accuracy only a finite
number of operators is needed, and the theory, although non-renormalizable in the traditional sense,
is predictive order by order in $E/\Lambda$. The price is a proliferation of parameters. Truncating
at a fixed order in $1/\Lambda$ leaves a finite but large set of coefficients, and much of the
physical content of the construction lies in how those coefficients are related to one another and
to experiment.

The counting has a subtlety that matters for what follows. In most observables the leading effect of
a dimension-six operator is its interference with the Standard Model amplitude, of order $c_i
E^2/\Lambda^2$, while the square of the new contribution is of order $c_i^2E^4/\Lambda^4$, the same
order as the interference of dimension-eight operators. Whether such squared terms are retained, as
in the linear and quadratic fits of global analyses, is therefore a statement about the truncation
of the expansion, and it matters most where the effects of the operators are largest, at high
energies. The same growth with the energy that makes the tails of kinematic distributions sensitive
to new physics thus also sets the limit of validity of the expansion, a point to which we return
below.

Writing down the operators is less straightforward than it may appear. Different operators can give
identical contributions to every observable, since they are related by integration by parts, by the
equations of motion of the light fields, by field redefinitions or, for fermions, by Fierz
identities. A basis is a maximal set of operators that remain independent once all these relations
are imposed, and only in such a basis are the Wilson coefficients uniquely defined. An equivalent
characterization, used throughout this thesis, identifies each independent operator with an
independent contact amplitude, that is, with a local kinematic structure consistent with the
helicities and the internal quantum numbers of the external
particles~\cite{Shadmi:2018xan,Durieux:2019eor,Dong:2021vxo,Li:2022tec}. This amplitude-operator
correspondence bypasses the redundancies of the Lagrangian altogether, since operators that differ
by a total derivative, by terms vanishing on shell or by a field redefinition generate the same
on-shell amplitudes.

The Standard Model Effective Field Theory
(SMEFT)~\cite{Buchmuller:1985jz,Grzadkowski:2010es,Brivio:2017vri,Isidori:2023pyp,Aebischer:2025qhh}
is the realization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:intro_Leff} built out of the Standard Model fields and invariant
under its gauge group, with the Higgs boson in a linearly realized $SU(2)_L$ doublet. Its only
operator at dimension five is the Weinberg operator, which generates Majorana masses for the
neutrinos after electroweak symmetry breaking~\cite{Weinberg:1979sa}. At dimension six, the
operators conserving baryon number reduce to $59$ independent structures for one generation of
fermions, collected in the Warsaw basis~\cite{Grzadkowski:2010es}, which become $2499$ independent
real parameters once the flavor structure of three generations is taken into
account~\cite{Alonso:2013hga}. The SMEFT assumes that no light states other than those of the
Standard Model exist below $\Lambda$, and it has become the reference framework for the combined
interpretation of collider, electroweak and flavor data.

That assumption is relaxed by the effective theory of axion-like particles (ALPs), which adds a
light pseudoscalar $\phi$ to the Standard
Model~\cite{Jaeckel:2010ni,Marsh:2015xka,Irastorza:2018dyq,DiLuzio:2020wdo}. A field of this kind is
expected whenever an approximate global symmetry is spontaneously broken at a scale well above the
electroweak one, since it is then the pseudo-Nambu--Goldstone boson of the broken symmetry, and its
lightness is protected by the symmetry itself. It extends the QCD
axion~\cite{Peccei:1977hh,Peccei:1977ur,Weinberg:1977ma,Wilczek:1977pj}, from which it differs in
that its mass and the scale of symmetry breaking are not tied to each other. ALPs bear on several of
the open questions listed above, including the strong CP problem, the flavor
puzzle~\cite{Davidson:1981zd,Wilczek:1982rv,Berezhiani:1989fp,Calibbi:2016hwq,Greljo:2024evt}, the
stability of the electroweak scale~\cite{Graham:2015cka} and the nature of dark
matter~\cite{Abbott:1982af,Preskill:1982cy,Dine:1982ah,Davis:1986xc}. Their Goldstone origin shapes
the way they couple to the Standard Model. An exact shift symmetry $\phi\to\phi+c$ allows couplings
only through the derivative $\partial_\mu\phi$ or through the products of $\phi$ with $F\widetilde
F$ and $G\widetilde G$, which change by a total derivative, so that the leading interactions arise
at dimension five~\cite{Georgi:1986df} and are suppressed by the symmetry-breaking scale. The
operators invariant under the shift symmetry are known up to dimension
eight~\cite{Song:2023lxf,Grojean:2023tsd,Bertuzzo:2023slg}. If the symmetry is also broken
explicitly, couplings such as $\phi FF$, $\phi GG$ and $\phi \overline f f$ become possible, and
since they have CP properties opposite to those of the shift-symmetric ones, their simultaneous
presence violates CP~\cite{Marciano:2016yhf,DiLuzio:2020oah}.

The ALP recurs in both halves of this thesis for a reason worth making explicit, since each half
exercises a different feature of its interactions. The mixing of its dimension-five couplings to
gauge bosons into its couplings to fermions requires the insertion of a fermion mass, and
controlling such mass insertions is one of the ingredients developed in the second part. Its
derivative couplings, on the other hand, make the amplitudes grow with the energy, more steeply as
the operator dimension increases, so that the ALP effective theory is particularly exposed to the
unitarity constraints studied in the third part.

Whichever effective theory is adopted, its coefficients are tied to the UV in two steps. In the
first, known as matching, the heavy states are integrated out at a scale $\mu\sim\Lambda$, and the
coefficients are fixed by requiring the effective theory to reproduce the low-energy amplitudes of
the full theory. In a top-down approach, where a specific UV model is considered, this determines
the $c_i(\Lambda)$ as functions of its couplings and masses. In a bottom-up approach, where no model
is assumed, the $c_i$ are treated as free parameters to be constrained by data, and matching is what
later translates those constraints into statements about specific models~\cite{deBlas:2017xtg}.
Matching enters this thesis mostly as a check of the bounds derived in its third part.

In the second step, known as running, the coefficients are evolved from $\mu\sim\Lambda$ down to the
scale $\mu\sim E$ of the measurement, according to the renormalization-group
equations~\cite{Callan:1970yg,Symanzik:1970rt}
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro_RGE}
    \mu\frac{\dd c_i}{\dd\mu} = \sum_j \gamma_{ij}\,c_j + \dots\,,
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{ij}$ is the anomalous-dimension matrix, which depends on the couplings and mass
parameters of the theory, and the ellipsis denotes terms of higher order in the Wilson coefficients,
arising from multiple insertions of higher-dimensional operators. Solving these equations resums the
large logarithms of $\Lambda/E$ that would otherwise spoil perturbation theory. The diagonal entries
of $\gamma_{ij}$ describe the multiplicative renormalization of each operator, while the
off-diagonal ones describe operator mixing, by which a coefficient generated at $\Lambda$ induces,
at lower energies, coefficients that were absent at the matching scale. Mixing is not restricted to
operators of the same dimension. Operators of different dimensions can mix whenever the difference
is compensated by the mass parameters of the theory, and products of lower-dimensional coefficients
can source higher-dimensional operators. Running is the central subject of the second part of this
thesis.

\bigskip

The first requirement, control over the evolution of the coefficients, therefore rests on the
anomalous-dimension matrix, which is a necessary ingredient for interpreting experimental results in
an effective theory. Its off-diagonal entries determine how a bound obtained on one operator
translates into constraints on the coefficients of others, and they can make an observable sensitive
to new physics that does not contribute to it at the matching scale. Electric dipole moments and
flavor-violating decays are typical examples, since they are measured at or below the GeV scale with
a precision that allows them to probe, through mixing, operators generated at the TeV scale or
above. The one-loop anomalous-dimension matrix of the dimension-six SMEFT is known in
full~\cite{Jenkins:2013zja,Jenkins:2013wua,Alonso:2013hga,Alonso:2014zka}, as is that of the
Low-Energy Effective Field Theory (LEFT), which describes the physics below the electroweak
scale~\cite{Jenkins:2017jig,Jenkins:2017dyc}, and the running of the ALP couplings has been studied
at one loop~\cite{Bauer:2020jbp,Chala:2020wvs,DasBakshi:2023lca}.

The ALP offers a concrete illustration of why mixing matters. When it is heavier than the scale of
the measurement and is integrated out, its CP-violating couplings generate dimension-six operators
such as the Weinberg operator and the electric and chromo-electric dipole operators of quarks and
leptons, which are in turn constrained by measurements of electric dipole moments at very low
energies. How strongly these measurements constrain the original ALP couplings depends on how the
generated operators run down to the hadronic scale, and on how they mix with one another along the
way.

These computations showed that anomalous-dimension matrices are much sparser than dimensional
analysis and power counting would suggest, since many mixings allowed by every symmetry of the
theory vanish at one loop. In the SMEFT the vanishing entries follow a pattern reminiscent of the
holomorphy of supersymmetric theories~\cite{Alonso:2014rga}, and several mechanisms have since been
identified behind them, from supersymmetric embeddings of the one-loop
computation~\cite{Elias-Miro:2014eia} to selection rules based on helicity~\cite{Cheung:2015aba}, on
the length of the operators~\cite{Bern:2019wie}, on angular momentum~\cite{Jiang:2020rwz} and on
color~\cite{Bern:2020ikv}. Diagrammatically these zeros are not manifest. They appear only once all
Feynman diagrams contributing to a given mixing are summed and the result, obtained in a Green's
basis that also contains operators vanishing on shell, is reduced to a basis of physical operators,
so that they take the form of cancellations among terms that are individually non-zero and gauge
dependent. In the on-shell language they follow instead from simple arguments, as discussed below,
and can be predicted before any loop computation is performed.

Whether comparable structures persist beyond one loop is a question of direct practical relevance.
The separation between a UV theory at or above the TeV scale and the observables that constrain it
most strongly, such as dipole moments and rare flavor transitions, spans several orders of
magnitude, and the accuracy of the corresponding measurements is such that two-loop running is
required in a growing number of
analyses~\cite{Allwicher:2023aql,Stefanek:2024kds,Haisch:2024wnw,Olgoso:2025jot,Mantani:2026fao,Born:2026tgm,Haisch:2026eyq}.
More fundamentally, whenever an entry of the anomalous-dimension matrix vanishes at one loop, the
two-loop term is not a correction but the leading contribution to the corresponding mixing. The
radiative decay $b\to s\gamma$ is a classic instance, since its rate is substantially enhanced by
the mixing of four-quark operators into the photon dipole operator, which arises first at two
loops~\cite{Buchalla:1995vs}. Knowing which two-loop entries vanish, and which are nevertheless
fixed by one-loop information, therefore determines which effects can be neglected and which must be
computed, at a time when two-loop calculations in effective theories are
multiplying~\cite{Aebischer:2022anv,Jenkins:2023bls,Born:2024mgz,DiNoi:2024ajj,Naterop:2024cfx,Ibarra:2024tpt,Guedes:2025sax,Duhr:2025zqw,Naterop:2025lzc,DiNoi:2025tka,Zhang:2025ywe,Born:2026xkr}.
The question is also subtler than at one loop. At one loop the anomalous dimensions do not depend on
the renormalization scheme, whereas from two loops onwards they do, since a finite change of scheme
at one loop feeds into the two-loop terms. Beyond one loop, moreover, loop-level objects enter the
unitarity cuts from which the selection rules are derived, and the question becomes which zeros
survive and in which sense they are independent of the scheme.

A further difficulty is that anomalous dimensions depend on the field content and on the gauge group
of the theory at hand. For renormalizable couplings this was addressed long ago by computing the
renormalization-group equations of a general gauge theory with arbitrary scalars and fermions, from
which those of any specific model follow by group-theoretic
manipulations~\cite{Machacek:1983tz,Machacek:1983fi,Machacek:1984zw}. Extending this program to
higher-dimensional operators is valuable for the same reason, and for a more specific one as well.
The SMEFT and the ALP effective theory are both special cases of a theory with scalars and fermions
in arbitrary representations of an arbitrary gauge group, together with its gauge bosons, and so are
many other extensions of the Standard Model by light states, such as additional $U(1)$ gauge bosons,
which can leave distinctive signatures through their mixing into the well-constrained operators of
the SMEFT~\cite{Galda:2021hbr}. A general framework makes such effects accessible for any of these
extensions once the group and the representations are specified, without a new loop computation.

\bigskip

The second requirement concerns the values the Wilson coefficients may take. Because the operators
in Eq.~\eqref{eq:intro_Leff} have dimension larger than four, the amplitudes they generate grow with
the energy, and at some point they become too large to be compatible with the conservation of
probability. Unitarity of the $S$-matrix, projected onto states of definite total angular momentum,
bounds each partial-wave amplitude by a number of order one. Since the partial-wave amplitude
generated by a dimension-six operator in a $2\to2$ process grows as $c_i s/\Lambda^2$, the bound
translates into an upper limit on $|c_i|$ that decreases with the energy, and the energy at which a
given coefficient saturates it marks the scale at which the effective description ceases to be
perturbatively reliable. Applied to the scattering of longitudinally polarized $W$ bosons, the same
reasoning led to the no-lose theorem, which bounded the Higgs mass at about $1$~TeV, or, had there
been no Higgs boson, placed the onset of new dynamics at that scale~\cite{Lee:1977yc,Lee:1977eg},
and which weighed heavily in the case for building the LHC.

In the effective-theory context, partial-wave unitarity bounds serve a related
purpose~\cite{Gounaris:1994cm,Corbett:2014ora,Corbett:2017qgl,DiLuzio:2016sur,DiLuzio:2017chi,Falkowski:2019tft,Chang:2019vez,Cohen:2021ucp,Brivio:2021fog,Cao:2023qks}.
As noted above, searches for new physics at colliders increasingly rely on the high-energy tails of
kinematic distributions, and the same enhancement that makes those tails sensitive makes the
truncation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:intro_Leff} questionable there. An interpretation of such data in terms
of Wilson coefficients therefore requires a quantitative statement of where the effective
description remains consistent, and unitarity bounds supply it from first principles, independently
of any experimental input. Being tree-level statements, they do not exclude regions of parameter
space in the strict sense. They indicate instead the energy above which the effective theory must be
completed, by new states or by strong dynamics, which is the information needed to assess the
validity of an effective-theory analysis.

The standard implementation of these bounds decomposes $2\to2$ helicity amplitudes into partial
waves by means of the Wigner $d$-matrices~\cite{Jacob:1959at}, and diagonalizes the matrix of
partial-wave amplitudes connecting all states with the same quantum numbers, imposing the unitarity
condition on its eigenvalues. This coupled-channel analysis is necessary, because coefficients that
enter the same partial wave are correlated and must be constrained together. Since the partial-wave
coefficients depend linearly on the Wilson coefficients, the unitarity conditions carve out a convex
region in coefficient space, and the bound on a single coefficient obtained by letting all the
others vary, known as a marginalized bound, is a projection of that region, conservative by
construction and distinct from the bound obtained by switching on one operator at a time. The method
is, however, tied to the kinematics of two-body states. Processes with more particles in the final
state, whose amplitudes often grow faster with the energy and are therefore expected to dominate at
the energies of the High-Luminosity LHC and of future
colliders~\cite{FCC:2018byv,Accettura:2023ked}, lie outside it. This matters most for operators that
generate no energy-growing $2\to2$ amplitude at all, the six-Higgs operator
$(\varphi^\dagger\varphi)^3$ of the SMEFT being the simplest example, which the standard analysis
cannot constrain. Even for $2\to2$ processes, moreover, the construction becomes impractical for
particles of spin two or higher, relevant for effective theories of gravity, because the
corresponding amplitudes are cumbersome to evaluate from Feynman rules.

Unitarity is not the only property of the $S$-matrix that constrains the Wilson coefficients.
Analyticity, which follows from causality, together with crossing symmetry relates the low-energy
amplitude to its behavior at all energies through dispersion relations. For forward elastic
scattering, the coefficient of $s^2$ at low energies is thereby expressed as an integral over the
total cross section, which is positive, provided the amplitude grows more slowly than $s^2$ at high
energies. When the effective theory is the low-energy limit of a unitary, local and causal
completion, this yields inequalities on specific combinations of coefficients, known as positivity
bounds~\cite{Adams:2006sv}. They are derived in Section~\ref{sec:analyticity} and apply most
directly to operators whose forward amplitudes grow as $s^2$, which in the theories of interest
arise at dimension eight, the quartic photon interactions of the Euler--Heisenberg Lagrangian being
a familiar example. At dimension six the forward dispersion relation does not fix a sign. For
four-fermion operators, however, spin-dependent dispersion relations, known as spinning sum rules,
relate the sign of certain combinations of coefficients to the spin of the heavy states exchanged in
the UV~\cite{Remmen:2020uze,Remmen:2022orj}, at the price of additional assumptions on the UV
completion. Positivity and unitarity bounds are complementary in character, since the former
restrict the signs of combinations of coefficients and the latter their magnitudes, so that the
region allowed by both can be considerably smaller than either alone.

These three classes of constraints rest on assumptions of increasing strength, which is worth
keeping in mind whenever they are compared or combined. Partial-wave unitarity bounds require only
that the effective theory be perturbatively consistent at the energy considered, and say nothing
about its UV completion. Positivity bounds require that some unitary, local and causal completion
exists, but not what it is. Spinning sum rules further assume a tree-level completion dominated by
states of definite spin, together with a high-energy behavior of the amplitude soft enough for the
contributions from infinity to vanish, which fails for instance when heavy vectors are exchanged in
the $t$ channel. In exchange for these assumptions they return a different kind of information,
whether the new dynamics is dominated by scalar or by vector states. A coefficient lying outside the
regions selected by the sum rules therefore does not exclude a UV completion, but points to a more
structured one, involving for instance vectors exchanged in the $t$ channel, loop effects or a
mixture of spins.

The practical interest of such constraints grows with the size of the parameter spaces involved.
With about $2500$ independent coefficients at dimension six, the SMEFT has far more parameters than
current data can constrain simultaneously, and global analyses~\cite{Brivio:2019ius,Celada:2024mcf}
benefit from criteria that restrict the viable region without assuming a specific model. Constraints
derived from first principles serve this purpose, and can be imposed directly in such analyses and
in the generation of simulated events.

\bigskip

The two programs outlined above share a common technical obstacle. Computations in effective field
theories become intricate rapidly, since the number of Feynman diagrams grows steeply with the
number of loops and external legs, and higher operator dimensions, multiple operator insertions and
external particles with spin each make the problem worse. The difficulty is not only one of size.
Individual diagrams depend on the choice of gauge and on how the fields are continued off shell,
only their sum being physical, and in the computation of anomalous dimensions the divergences must
first be absorbed in the Green's basis and then projected onto the physical one through field
redefinitions.

On-shell methods remove these intermediate steps by never leaving the space of physical amplitudes.
Their ingredients are gauge-invariant functions of the external momenta and helicities, constrained
by unitarity, locality and
analyticity~\cite{Mangano:1990by,Dixon:1996wi,Elvang:2013cua,Dixon:2013uaa,Cheung:2017pzi,Travaglini:2022uwo}.
In this work, the decisive feature is less their efficiency than the fact that a single property of
the $S$-matrix gives access to both of the questions addressed in this thesis.

Unitarity relates the discontinuity of an amplitude to products of on-shell amplitudes of lower loop
order~\cite{Bern:1994zx,Bern:1994cg,Britto:2004nc}. A unitarity cut puts the intermediate states on
shell, so that the discontinuity is assembled from lower-order physical amplitudes alone. The same
holds for the form factors of the operators, that is, for their matrix elements between the vacuum
and on-shell multi-particle states, and here the discontinuity has a direct physical meaning, since
the action of the dilatation operator on a form factor is fixed by the phase of the $S$-matrix, so
that anomalous dimensions can be read off from unitarity
cuts~\cite{Caron-Huot:2016cwu,EliasMiro:2020tdv,Baratella:2020lzz,Jiang:2020mhe,Bern:2020ikv}. The
renormalization-group equations of the second part of this thesis are obtained in this way, as
phase-space integrals of products of tree-level amplitudes and form factors. Read differently, the
same discontinuity gives the bounds of the third part. Projected onto states of definite angular
momentum, unitarity bounds the partial-wave coefficients, while combined with analyticity through a
dispersion relation it confines the Wilson coefficients to a convex region. A single object, the
unitarity cut of an on-shell amplitude, thus governs both how the Wilson coefficients evolve with
the energy and where they are allowed to be.

The formalism also has features that shape the results of the second part, one practical and one
limiting. Unitarity-based methods are most naturally formulated for massless particles, whereas many
phenomenologically relevant mixings, for instance between operators of opposite chirality, require
insertions of fermion masses. The Higgs low-energy theorem~\cite{Ellis:1975ap,Shifman:1979eb} allows
these leading mass effects to be captured by insertions of a zero-momentum Higgs boson, so that the
formalism can remain massless throughout. On the other hand, four-dimensional unitarity cuts do not
determine the finite rational terms of one-loop amplitudes and form factors, which have no
discontinuity~\cite{Bern:1994zx,Bern:1994cg}. This is what divides the two-loop analysis into zeros
that are fixed by tree-level data and hold in any scheme that preserves the length selection rule,
and zeros that depend on the scheme, whose derivation requires in addition an assumption on the
behavior of one-loop rational terms at complex-collinear kinematics.

Within these limits, working with physical amplitudes changes what can be seen. Following
Ref.~\cite{Cheung:2015aba}, one can assign to each operator a pair of weights $(w,\overline
w)=(\ell-h,\ell+h)$, where the length $\ell$ is the number of particles in the simplest amplitude
the operator generates and $h$ is their total helicity. The weights of the amplitudes entering a
unitarity cut combine in a definite way, and the tree-level amplitudes of the renormalizable theory
with four or more legs have $w,\overline w\ge4$, so that at one loop an operator renormalizes only
operators whose weights are at least as large as its own, up to a shift by two units in the presence
of Yukawa couplings of both holomorphic and anti-holomorphic type. Four-fermion operators, for
instance, cannot renormalize the operators built from three field strengths, and the sparsity of
anomalous-dimension matrices discussed above becomes a consequence of counting. Operators related by
a symmetry, such as those differing only in their CP properties, acquire anomalous dimensions that
are manifestly tied together, a relation hidden in the diagrammatic approach, where such operators
have unrelated Feynman rules. For the unitarity bounds the gain is one of reach. Since the
angular-momentum content of an amplitude can be read off from its spinor-helicity representation,
the partial-wave decomposition can be constructed directly, for any number of external particles and
any spin, by diagonalizing a Casimir operator of the Poincaré group, the square of the
Pauli--Lubanski vector, on independent kinematic structures~\cite{Jiang:2020rwz,Shu:2021qlr}.
Together with the amplitude-operator correspondence, this ensures that once processes of all the
relevant multiplicities are included, every direction in the space of Wilson coefficients is
constrained.

The two halves of the thesis are related by more than a shared set of tools. Positivity, which in
the third part constrains the values of the Wilson coefficients, also constrains their running,
since the bounds must hold at every renormalization scale. This forbids certain operator mixings and
fixes the sign of certain dimension-eight anomalous
dimensions~\cite{Chala:2023jyx,Chala:2023xjy,Liao:2025npz}, and an instance of the same consistency
appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:ALP_bounds}, where a positivity bound on dimension-eight operators is
found to be compatible with the sign of the running induced by pairs of dimension-six operators. The
zeros of the anomalous-dimension matrix and the positivity bounds have different origins, the former
in helicity and length counting and the latter in analyticity, but they share a feature. Both are
structures in the space of Wilson coefficients that are invisible at the level of the Lagrangian and
become manifest on shell, and the results collected in this thesis explore them from both sides.

The on-shell formalism required in the following chapters is collected in
Chapter~\ref{ch:amplitudes}. It introduces the spinor-helicity variables and the polarizations of
massless states up to spin two, discusses unitarity, locality and analyticity of the $S$-matrix,
including the derivation of the dispersion relations and positivity bounds used in the third part,
and illustrates how these principles fix three-particle amplitudes and determine higher-point ones
recursively. The remaining technical ingredients, such as form factors, the relation between the
dilatation operator and the $S$-matrix, and the angular-momentum basis, are introduced in the
chapters where they are first needed.

\bigskip

The thesis is organized in four parts. The first, of which this Introduction is the opening chapter,
sets out the motivation and the tools, and continues with the review of on-shell methods in
Chapter~\ref{ch:amplitudes}. The second and third parts contain the original results. Each of their
chapters is based on one or two of the articles listed at the beginning of this thesis, as indicated
at its opening, is written so as to be readable on its own, and collects its technical material in
appendices at its end. The fourth part contains the conclusions.

The second part is devoted to the anomalous dimensions of effective field theories.
Chapter~\ref{ch:RGEmethod}, based on Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2023jsu}, reviews the relation between the
dilatation operator and the phase of the $S$-matrix and derives from it master formulae for the
anomalous dimensions at one and two loops, valid for the most general mixing among operators of
different dimensions and for multiple operator insertions. It also shows how leading mass effects
can be included through the Higgs low-energy theorem while the calculation remains massless. These
formulae are the common tool of the three chapters that follow. Chapter~\ref{ch:ALP_RGEs}, based on
Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2024shu}, applies them to the one-loop renormalization of a CP-violating ALP
coupled to Standard Model fields, above and below the electroweak scale, including the dimension-six
operators generated once the ALP is integrated out, and compares the on-shell computation with a
parallel one based on Feynman diagrams. Chapter~\ref{ch:generalEFT}, based on
Refs.~\cite{Aebischer:2025zxg,Aebischer:2025ddl}, turns to a general effective gauge theory. We
construct its physical operator basis up to dimension six and derive the complete one-loop
renormalization-group equations for all its couplings at order $1/\Lambda^2$, from which those of a
specific theory follow by group-theoretic manipulations alone. The SMEFT, the LEFT and the $O(n)$
model serve both as cross-checks and as worked examples. Chapter~\ref{ch:2looptheorems}, based on
Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2026yuw}, asks which structure of the anomalous-dimension matrix survives
beyond one loop. We show that helicity selection rules fix to zero, from tree-level data alone, the
$L$-loop mixings of operators into operators $L-1$ units shorter whenever the weights differ
sufficiently, a result that holds in every scheme preserving the length selection rule. For targets
at least as long as the source, and assuming complex-collinear factorization, we construct a scheme
in which further two-loop zeros hold, and show that in any other scheme the same entries are
determined by one-loop data.

The third part is devoted to theoretical constraints on the Wilson coefficients.
Chapter~\ref{ch:generalizedUB}, based on Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2025toe}, reformulates the
partial-wave decomposition so that partial-wave coefficients become inner products between
amplitudes and angular-momentum basis elements, the latter obtained by diagonalizing the square of
the Pauli--Lubanski operator on spinor-helicity monomials. This extends partial-wave unitarity
bounds to processes with arbitrary multiplicity and spin. We apply it to the quartic interactions of
photons and gravitons, comparing the resulting bounds with positivity bounds, and to $2\to3$
processes in the SMEFT, identifying the energies above which they supersede the $2\to2$ ones.
Chapter~\ref{ch:ALP_bounds}, based on Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2025ojh}, applies the formalism to the
ALP effective theory up to dimension eight, including couplings to leptons that do not respect the
$SU(2)_L$ symmetry, derives the complete set of positivity bounds on its dimension-eight operators,
and compares both with collider searches and rare decays. Chapter~\ref{ch:SMEFT_bounds}, based on
Ref.~\cite{Bresciani:2026acy} and on a companion article, performs a coupled-channel analysis of the
dimension-six SMEFT over all helicity, gauge and flavor configurations, marginalizing each bound
over the remaining coefficients. It derives spinning sum rules for four-fermion operators in the
Warsaw basis together with a criterion to impose them in flavor space, extends the bounds to the
minimal-flavor-violation~\cite{DAmbrosio:2002vsn,Cirigliano:2005ck} and
$U(2)^5$~\cite{Allwicher:2023shc} hypotheses on the flavor structure, and compares them with current
and projected experimental limits. The fourth part summarizes the results and discusses possible
extensions.

A few remarks may help the reader navigate the material. Chapter~\ref{ch:RGEmethod} is a
prerequisite for the rest of the second part, and Chapter~\ref{ch:2looptheorems} relies in
particular on its two-loop master formula, while Chapter~\ref{ch:generalizedUB} is a prerequisite
for the rest of the third part, whose positivity bounds also draw on Section~\ref{sec:analyticity}.
Several chapters contain extensive collections of results intended for consultation more than for
continuous reading. This is the case for the class-by-class derivation of the anomalous dimensions
of the ALP effective theory in Section~\ref{sec:ALPuvanomalousdim}, for the renormalization-group
equations of the general effective gauge theory in Section~\ref{sec:gEFTresults}, for the tables of
two-loop zeros in Section~\ref{sec:2loopapplications}, and for the bounds on flavorful four-fermion
operators in Appendix~\ref{app:SMEFT_flavorresults}. As for the scope, the anomalous dimensions
computed explicitly are one-loop quantities, while the statements about two loops and beyond concern
the structure of the anomalous-dimension matrix, and the general effective gauge theory is treated
at order $1/\Lambda^2$ for linearly realized gauge symmetries. The unitarity and positivity bounds
are derived at tree level, and the regime of validity of the sum rules is discussed explicitly where
they are used.
